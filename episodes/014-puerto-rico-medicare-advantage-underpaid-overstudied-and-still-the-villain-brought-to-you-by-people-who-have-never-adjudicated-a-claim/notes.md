A **2026** study in *Health Affairs Scholar* opened the books of the three
insurers that run Medicare Advantage in Puerto Rico, MMM, Triple-S and MCS,
from **2011 to 2022**, and concluded that the island's plans are a profitable
oligopoly living on public money while asking for more of it. This episode
reads the same ledger and asks who wrote the first draft. The funding gap is
real: benchmark payments about **38%** below the states, about **26%** after
risk scores and quality bonuses. But the plans pay the island's physicians
about **40%** less than the states pay theirs, **99%** of Puerto Rico's
beneficiaries sit in plans CMS rates **4.5 stars** or better, and the plans
clear the **85-cent** medical loss ratio floor that the Affordable Care Act
wrote into law. Underpaid, overstudied, and still cast as the villain, by
people who have never adjudicated a claim.

In this episode:

- **Two readings of one balance sheet.** On the mainland about **54%** of
  Medicare beneficiaries choose Medicare Advantage; in Puerto Rico it is
  **95%**, the de facto health system for older adults on the archipelago.
  The insurers say Washington starves them; the study says they are a
  profitable oligopoly sustained by public funds. Same numbers, opposite
  conclusions.
- **The gap, and the cushion under it.** Risk-standardized benchmark payments
  to Puerto Rico plans run about **38%** below the states, the number the
  Medicaid and Medicare Advantage Products Association has carried to
  Congress. Higher risk scores and quality bonus payments shrink the gap to
  roughly **26%**. Physicians on the island are reimbursed about **40%** less
  than their mainland peers, and that 14-point difference is the buffer the
  plans live on. The squeeze does not disappear; it rolls downhill to the
  providers.
- **Twelve years of receipts.** MMM, Triple-S and MCS covered about **92%** of
  the island's Medicare beneficiaries by 2022, with mean annual revenues of
  **$4.92 billion**. The study tracks three lines: the medical loss ratio
  (what goes to care), the administrative loss ratio (what goes to overhead)
  and the margin (what is left for the house).
- **The 85-cent rule, met.** Since **2014** the ACA has required Medicare
  Advantage plans to spend at least 85 cents of every dollar on care. The
  study finds that the funding disparity was not significantly associated
  with the medical loss ratio and treats that as a market failure. The plans
  read it as compliance: the care floor held, and 99% of beneficiaries are on
  plans CMS rates 4.5 stars or higher. The only line the gap tracked was
  administrative spending, which is where a manager cuts when the check
  shrinks.
- **Disaster economics.** Capitation pays a flat fee per member per month
  whether or not care is used. In **2017** Hurricane María closed the roads
  and the clinics; in **2020** the pandemic closed everything else.
  Utilization collapsed while the payments kept coming: the medical loss
  ratio hit a study-period low of **80.1%** in 2020 and margins peaked at
  **3.32%**. The gym kept the membership fee while the doors were locked.
- **What normal looks like.** In **2022**, with utilization back and the local
  insurers absorbed by national corporate owners, the margin fell to
  **negative 0.19%**. Under ordinary conditions the oligopoly's profit is a
  rounding error.
- **The receipts.** For Washington: if new money arrives to close the 38%
  gap, fence it with a **90%** medical loss ratio floor on the additional
  dollars and audit it, but recognize that the plans are holding together a
  system Congress left underfunded. For the plans: transparent accounting of
  vertical integration, and a clear rule for what counts as a medical expense
  when the insurer pays its own clinics and pharmacies. For beneficiaries: a
  traditional Medicare option with lower out-of-pocket costs, so that 95%
  becomes a choice and not a default.

The closing question: if a financing model like capitation reaches its
highest profitability during a population's worst months, a hurricane, a
pandemic, is the system broken, or is it working exactly as its incentives
were designed? Puerto Rico is the extreme case because of that 95%, but the
arithmetic is national. Something to mull over the next time the premium
clears.
