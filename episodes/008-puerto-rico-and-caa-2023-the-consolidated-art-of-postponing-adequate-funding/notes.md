Hidden inside the everyday machinery of the U.S. healthcare system is a
multi-billion-dollar legislative clock, and its alarm is set for **October 1,
2027**. When it goes off, the Medicaid funding cap for Puerto Rico is
recalculated as if the last eight years of federal support never happened.
This episode takes the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 (CAA 2023)
apart, mechanism by mechanism: the wound it was supposed to treat, the
five-year band-aid it applied, the cliff it wrote into the same law, and the
permanent fix it granted to every other U.S. territory except the largest and
poorest one.

In this episode:

- **The wound.** In the 50 states, federal Medicaid matching is open-ended and
  tracks per-capita income, from 50% up to **83%** for the poorest state.
  Puerto Rico's Plan Vital runs under a hard annual cap and a matching rate
  fixed by statute at **55%**, on an island with a poverty rate of roughly
  40% to 43% (Mississippi, the poorest state, is about 19%) where nearly half
  the population depends on Medicaid and CHIP. Result: **$1,171** in federal
  Medicaid spending per person in Puerto Rico versus **$1,837** nationally,
  and no nursing-facility benefit, minimal non-emergency transport, and no
  full Medicare Savings Program.
- **The band-aid.** CAA 2023 did not change the framework; it bought five
  years. Allocations rose from **$3.275 billion** in FY2023 to **$3.825
  billion** in FY2027, the match went from 55% to **76%**, and two conditional
  bonuses were attached: **$300 million** a year for a directed-payment
  arrangement that forces managed-care organizations to pay providers at
  least **75% of the Medicare Part B rate**, and **$75 million** a year for
  program-integrity leads. Puerto Rico met every condition, unlocking more than
  **$19 billion** in potential five-year relief.
- **The cliff.** For FY2028 the law resets the cap to the 2019 baseline, about
  $367 million plus inflation. The Congressional Budget Office projects the
  annual cap falling from roughly **$4 billion to $500 million**, with the
  match snapping back to 55%. Why design it that way? The ten-year CBO scoring
  window: a permanent fix looks expensive on paper, an expiring one looks
  cheap. Projected fallout if nothing changes: **$3.5 billion** in federal
  funding lost overnight, a **$5.4 billion** economic contraction, **48,000
  jobs**, and **700,000 to 1 million** people losing coverage, pushing the
  uninsured rate from 5.7% to nearly 30%.
- **The asymmetry.** The same law made the **83%** match permanent for Guam,
  the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands and
  turned their elevated caps into a permanent, inflation-indexed baseline. No
  cliff. Puerto Rico, 91% of all territorial Medicaid spending, was left on the
  temporary track.
- **What CAA 2023 changed for the 50 states.** Twelve months of continuous
  eligibility for children, a permanent option for 12 months of postpartum
  coverage instead of 60 days, suspension rather than termination of Medicaid
  for incarcerated juveniles with screenings and case management before
  release, an $8 million crisis-services assistance center, the phase-down of
  the 6.2% pandemic match with monthly redetermination reporting, and
  quarterly-updated provider directories to end ghost networks.
- **The playbook.** For Puerto Rico's government: a unified, data-driven front
  in Washington under Executive Order 2026-006, seeking a permanent 83% match
  and a sustainable **$4.4 billion** base before the FY2028 recalculation. For
  Congress: align Puerto Rico with the permanent fix the other four territories
  already have. For providers and plans: lock in the 75% Part B floor while
  the money is there and model late-2027 volatility. For beneficiaries on the
  island: the asset verification system that went live on January 1, 2026,
  with a 60-month look-back.

The closing question: if Congress lets the 2028 cliff happen and up to a
million American citizens in Puerto Rico lose their coverage or their jobs,
what happens to the mainland healthcare system when tens of thousands of them
move to the 50 states looking for the care they can no longer get at home? A
localized policy cliff rarely stays localized.
