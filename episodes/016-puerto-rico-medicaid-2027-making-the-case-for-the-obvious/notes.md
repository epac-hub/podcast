Roughly **40%** of Puerto Rico relies on Medicaid, almost double the **21%** average
across the 50 states, and the program runs on a capped Section 1108 allotment instead
of the open-ended federal match every state gets. The temporary fix from the
Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 lifted the island's matching rate from
**55%** to **76%** and raised the ceiling to **$3.825 billion** by fiscal year 2027.
When it expires, the match falls back to **55%**, the ceiling drops to a 2019
baseline, and about **$3.36 billion** a year leaves the system. This episode reads
the October 2026 report of the multi-sectoral Medicaid task force, the board that
has to make the case for the obvious one more time: between **860,000** and
**1 million** people could lose coverage, and underfunding the island already costs
federal taxpayers more than funding it would.

In this episode:

- **An open tab versus a gift card.** In the states, federal Medicaid money is
  open-ended: the government matches allowable costs with no cap, up to **83%**
  for the poorest states. Puerto Rico gets a fixed allotment with a statutory base
  match of **55%**. When the allotment runs out, every dollar after that is local.
  The 2023 boost let the island raise eligibility to **100%** of the federal
  poverty level instead of a local, artificially low one, but expenditures for
  2026 and 2027 are still projected to exceed even the higher cap.
- **Fewer people, sicker people.** The population fell almost **12%** in a decade
  and the population under 18 fell nearly **38%**, but **24.6%** of residents are
  65 or older against **18%** in the states. Poverty is **40.5%** against
  **12.5%**; **60% to 70%** of adults live with at least one chronic condition,
  hypertension runs **40% to 45%**, and diabetes **17.8%**. The healthy working-age
  taxpayers leave; the high-need patients stay. Of more than **81,000** people
  diagnosed with invasive cancer between 2019 and 2023, **42%** relied on Medicaid
  in their first year of treatment.
- **$74 a month.** U.S. citizens in Puerto Rico are excluded by law from
  Supplemental Security Income. The island's substitute, Aid to the Aged, Blind
  and Disabled, is a capped grant Puerto Rico must match **25%**, and it pays an
  average of about **$74** a month. The same citizen on SSI in the states could
  receive up to **$994**. **46.1%** of Puerto Ricans with a disability live below
  the poverty line, against **20.1%** stateside.
- **No long-term care, so the hospital is the long-term care.** Medicaid funding
  limits mean no home- and community-based long-term services and supports: no
  personal care aides, no residential services for adults with intellectual
  disabilities. Family members quit their jobs to provide care, or the condition
  waits until it becomes an emergency. The island's only trauma hospital runs a
  **-3.7%** net margin; the average U.S. hospital earns **+6.7%**.
- **The migration premium.** In a 2023 Ipsos survey of adults who had recently
  moved to the mainland, **19%** named health services among their top five
  reasons and **14%** moved specifically to treat a health condition. Medicaid
  spending per enrollee was **$3,293** in Puerto Rico in 2024 against **$10,377**
  in the median state. The **15,303** Medicaid-eligible people who relocated that
  year cost the federal government an estimated **$96.8 million** on the mainland,
  against **$41.8 million** had they been covered at home: an extra **$55 million**
  in a single year, **2.3** times the price for the same citizens.
- **The 2027 cliff.** In fiscal year 2028 the island's uninsured rate would rise
  from **5.7%** to roughly **30%**, and the services added in 2024, home health,
  durable medical equipment, hospice and non-emergency transportation, would be
  the first at risk.
- **Not a leaky bucket.** Puerto Rico has met every CMS program integrity
  requirement. It launched an enterprise data warehouse in February 2026, verifies
  applicants' assets electronically with financial institutions, pays hospitals by
  diagnosis-related group instead of line item, and earns an extra **$375 million**
  a year from the same federal government for its compliance. It now pays
  physicians **100%** of the Medicare Part B fee schedule to slow the brain drain.
  The task force asks Congress to remove the Section 1108 ceiling, apply the
  standard state formula, which would give Puerto Rico an **83%** match, and
  include the island in disproportionate share hospital payments.

The closing question: if capping Puerto Rico's Medicaid only moves the same
citizens to states where their care costs the federal government two to three
times as much, is the cap saving money, or just hiding the true cost of care on
someone else's balance sheet?
