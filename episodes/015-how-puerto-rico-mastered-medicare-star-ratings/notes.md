CMS rewrote the Medicare Advantage test for **2027**, and the industry called the
result a bloodbath. Of the **508** contracts rated, the enrollment-weighted average
fell to **3.99** stars, from **4.01** in 2026 and **4.07** in 2024. Only about
**37%** of plans reached four stars or better, down from **44%**, and just **15**
plans in the entire country earned a perfect five, down from **22**. Fourteen of
those fifteen are for-profit, and two of them are in Puerto Rico. Between them,
MCS Classicare and Triple-S Advantage put more than **481,000** people in
five-star Medicare Advantage care, more than every mainland five-star plan
combined, on benchmark payments far below the states. This episode reads the CMS
fact sheet, the Becker's Payer Issues breakdown, and the local reporting in
El Nuevo Día and Sin Comillas, and asks how the island cracked a test the
mainland is failing.

In this episode:

- **The test got harder, not the plans worse.** CMS rates each contract on up to
  **43** measures, from diabetes and blood pressure control to appeal rates,
  wait times, and interpreters at the call center. For 2027 it added a measure
  for patients prescribed an opioid and a benzodiazepine at the same time, a
  combination that can suppress breathing, and one for older adults taking
  several anticholinergic drugs, common antihistamines, antidepressants and
  bladder medications whose overlap can mimic dementia. The plan sits above
  every prescriber and every pharmacy claim, so the plan is the one that has to
  catch it.
- **Tripled weight on outcomes.** The measures that track whether members
  actually improve or maintain their physical and mental health went from a
  weight of **1** to **3** in the formula. A polite call center and a scheduled
  screening no longer carry a plan; the members have to be measurably
  healthier over time.
- **Non-profits win the average, for-profits win the peak.** About **44%** of
  non-profit contracts earned four stars or better against **34%** of
  for-profit contracts. But **14** of the **15** five-star plans are for-profit.
  The divide is not the size of the bank account. It is agility: how fast an
  organization can rebuild its data capture when CMS changes the math, in one
  fiscal year rather than three years of committees.
- **Two of the fifteen are on the island.** MCS Classicare earned five stars for
  the **fourth consecutive year**, the only plan in Puerto Rico to hold the
  maximum score year after year, with **360,519** members. Triple-S Advantage
  earned five stars on its HMO contract, **120,943** members, and **4.5** on
  its more flexible PPO, where the plan sees less of what the patient does.
- **More five-star patients than the rest of the country combined.** The
  mainland five-star plans, from UnitedHealthcare and Anthem to Kaiser in
  Nevada, Leon Health, Peak Health and Clover Health, add up to roughly
  **305,000** members. Puerto Rico's two contracts hold **481,000**. Nationwide
  **71%** of Medicare Advantage members are in four-star-or-better contracts;
  on the island nearly **100%** are in plans rated 4.5 stars or higher.
- **How they did it.** MCS's chief medical officer, Dr. Inés Hernández, says
  the result proves top execution is possible under the most challenging
  economic conditions in the United States; CEO Jim O'Drobinak points to
  alliances with the island's providers, and Triple-S Advantage credits its
  relationships with physicians and community organizations. The mechanism is
  human infrastructure as the data layer: a community health worker at the
  kitchen table looking at the pill bottles, coordinating with the primary care
  doctor and the pharmacist, and arranging the ride to the screening, instead
  of an automated text message nobody reads.

The closing question: if a jurisdiction with some of the lowest federal
reimbursement in the system can build a network where nearly half a million
people receive the highest rated Medicare Advantage care in the country, is
five-star healthcare about limitless funding, or about boots on the ground? And
what would the rest of the United States learn by treating Puerto Rico not as
an outlier but as the blueprint?
